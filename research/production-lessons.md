# Production lessons: cutting model cost at equal or better quality

What public write-ups from teams that run language models in production found about cutting model cost while keeping or raising quality, and what this library does with each lesson. This version reads Harvey's engineering blog, Uber's engineering post on running its software factory with two press reports on Uber, and eleven write-ups by others: four on prompt caching; three on routing, panels and a distilled model: a research lab's learned router, a vendor's panel of models and a team's small model trained for one high-volume job; Grab's on the gateway in front of its model providers and GitHub's on how it evaluates models for Copilot; and two analyses of how fast the price of a given benchmark score has fallen, by a venture firm and a research group.

Every quote below was checked word for word against its page as fetched on 2026-11-21, and each is under 40 words. A fact whose page could not be fetched is left out. A page paywalled past its opening is quoted only from the opening its fetch returned, and its Sources item says so. A page whose text fetch left out the part quoted is quoted from its raw HTML, and its Sources item says so. A number read from a figure is the figure's own label, read from the image fetched with its page. A number this note computes from a source's own numbers says it is computed here. The numbers are each author's own, measured on its own tasks, models and prices with data it does not publish, so this repository reproduces none of them; its own evidence is the committed results under `evidence/`. A model name, in a quote or in the text, is its author's product name, not this library's `vendor/model` ID. A result measured on a benchmark rather than on production traffic says so, and says who ran it.

Each lesson gives the mechanism, the result with its source, and what this library does with it: what it does today, and the change that would turn the lesson into routing behavior or measurement, or why the library does not take it. A change named here is planned, not shipped, unless the text says the library does it today.

## What the sources do not say

- No Harvey post read here says that Harvey first sent everything to frontier models and then hit a cost wall. The posts say that frontier agents cost too much and run too slowly for production legal work, that Harvey ran on OpenAI models before it added others, and that it moved high-volume work to smaller, faster or self-trained models.
- None of the Harvey posts gives a cost saving from routing between models as such. The cuts they give come from trained models, harness changes and cheaper graders, each against a baseline the post names.
- Bloomberg reported on 2026-06-02 that Uber capped each employee at $1,500 a month in token spending per AI coding tool; Uber's post of 2026-08-27 describes one shared spend tier across its interactive tools, with live counters and nudges, "To avoid imposing strict caps" ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)). These are two policies at two dates: no source read here says when, or whether, one replaced the other.
- Uber's 9.4x is weekly agentic requests from February to August, "weekly agentic requests grew 9.4x" ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)), while its total spend has held about level since April: "our total AI spend has relatively stabilized since April due to optimizations across the board." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)) The two have different baselines, so no saving per request is derived from them here; the post's own unit-cost cuts, with one model held fixed, are in the Uber lesson on holding the model fixed.
- Axios wrote that Uber "caps interactive sessions at 400,000 tokens, even when using a model that can handle up to one million tokens." ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/08/27/ai-uber-spending)) The post describes automatic compaction at 400k tokens, a threshold on the context, not a limit on a session's tokens or spend.
- None of the four write-ups on prompt caching moves work to another model: each saving they give comes with the model held fixed, from serving the same prompt prefix from the cache, so none of them is a saving from routing. Neradot lists a cheaper model for the easy calls among the steps it takes by default, "Consider a cheaper model." ([Neradot](https://www.neradot.com/post/cutting-inference-cost-36-percent-with-prompt-caching)), and gives no number for it.
- Kern AI's "Effective reduction: ~10x on input costs." ([Kern AI](https://kern-ai.com/blog/prompt-caching)) is on input tokens alone. By its own counts, about 10k of the 600k tokens at full price and 590k at 10%, the cut computed here is about 8.7x on input, before any cache-write premium, and the post gives no output cost or total bill.
- The Hacker News post is one developer's account of the bill for that developer's own coding agent, not a company write-up, and it gives no cache hit rate. Kern AI's post is about kern, an open-source agent, measured on "a real agent running 100k+ token prompts on Anthropic (Claude Opus via OpenRouter)" ([Kern AI](https://kern-ai.com/blog/prompt-caching)), with no company or traffic named.
- RouteLLM's numbers are a research lab's results on three public benchmarks, MT Bench, MMLU and GSM8K, for a router choosing between two models, not results on production traffic. Its cost is the share of calls sent to the strong model, "which is representative of the cost incurred since the cost of a Mixtral call is negligible" ([RouteLLM](https://lmsys.org/blog/2024-07-01-routellm/)), not a bill, and its quality level is a share of the strong model's score, "95% of GPT-4 performance" ([RouteLLM](https://lmsys.org/blog/2024-07-01-routellm/)), not a bar on success.
- Fusion's numbers are a vendor's benchmark of its own product, OpenRouter's on 100 DRACO deep-research tasks, not customer production data. Fable 5 alone and the panel that holds it were scored on 93 of the tasks, "7 of the 100 DRACO tasks were not completed because Fable 5’s content filters blocked them from executing." ([Fusion](https://openrouter.ai/blog/announcements/fusion-beats-frontier/)), so its two rows, the top panel and the best single model, were scored on fewer tasks than every other row; a judge other than the benchmark paper's means "our scores are not directly comparable to the original paper’s published results." ([Fusion](https://openrouter.ai/blog/announcements/fusion-beats-frontier/)); and the post gives a cost only for the budget panel, as a share of Fable 5's, with no price or token count.
- Replit's per-model scores are in chart images only, so this note gives no gap in score between its model and the others, and the post gives no cost or latency for its model against the API models. It was written before the model ran in production: "Once the model is in production, we will experiment with post-training methods like DPO leveraging user data collected by the Replit platform" ([code repair](https://blog.replit.com/code-repair)).
- Neither Grab's post nor GitHub's gives a number for what its gateway or its evaluation saved or caught. Grab's numbers are counts of use cases and models, with no cost figure; GitHub's are counts of tests, repositories and questions, with no score, cost or number of the drops its daily runs found.
- a16z's and Epoch AI's rates are analyses of public list prices and benchmark scores, by a venture firm and a research group, not any team's bill and not one model's price over time: each tracks the cheapest model that reaches a fixed score. a16z searched three model families only, "we limited our search to models from OpenAI, Anthropic, and Meta’s Llama from third party inference providers" ([a16z](https://a16z.com/llmflation-llm-inference-cost/)), and says "the methodology is far from perfect" ([a16z](https://a16z.com/llmflation-llm-inference-cost/)); Epoch AI left reasoning models out, "We excluded reasoning models from our analysis of per-token prices." ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/llm-inference-price-trends)) So no rate from them is applied here to any model's price.

## Harvey

### No single best model: route per task and per step

- Mechanism: Harvey added Anthropic and Google models to its OpenAI ones, routes to a model automatically by default, lets a user pick one, and chooses a model for each step of a workflow, "independently choosing the model best suited to plan, orchestrate, and execute each task across a complex system." ([model offerings](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/expanding-harveys-model-offerings))
- Result: "there is no longer a single “best” model, but an array of premier models, each particularly suited to different legal tasks and preferences." ([model offerings](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/expanding-harveys-model-offerings)) The same post: "Even on a relatively straightforward three-step process, completing 80% of the work at each step means only completing about half of the overall work." ([model offerings](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/expanding-harveys-model-offerings))
- In this library: it routes per task today. Each task has its own bar, and the router takes the cheapest model whose Wilson lower bound clears it. It sets no bar for a chain of steps, and three steps that each pass 0.8 of the time pass about 0.51 together. The change: a bar for a whole multi-step workflow, met by a combination of step routes admitted on whole-workflow outcomes, so a step may need a stronger model than its own bar asks for.

### Count the cost of finished work, not of one call

- Mechanism: Harvey states cost per Review Table cell, per data room and per query, and "But cost is a function of both token prices and tokens used." ([Tenet update](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/post-training-update-harvey-tenet))
- Result: for the custom Review Table model, "average cost per cell fell by 54.8% compared to Claude Sonnet 5 on the same tasks." ([Review Table models](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/training-frontier-review-table-models-with-applied-compute)) Its answer quality is set against other models in a sentence of its own: "the AC Review Table model reached 0.903 on our benchmark, compared with 0.867 for Fable 5 and 0.857 for GPT-5.6-Sol." ([Review Table models](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/training-frontier-review-table-models-with-applied-compute)) So the cost cut and the quality gain have different baselines.
- In this library: `cost_effective` prices a call at the model's list prices and its mean tokens per call on the task (the task's expected tokens until the stats hold token counts), and ranks only the models that clear the bar. Where a failed call is retried, the cheapest model per call can cost the most per success, so a task whose failed calls are retried sets `failure_policy` to `retry_same`, and the models that clear the bar are then ranked by expected cost per success, a call's cost over its Wilson lower bound, an estimate that assumes independent retries; the decision names `cheaper_per_success` when that changes the pick. With an audit file of one `call_id` and `correct` per call, `report --audits` gives each group's cost per success, every attempt of its calls over the calls an audit marked correct, and `n/a` until every call of the group is audited.

### Quality per token

- Mechanism: "intelligence-per-token, which is measured as the mean rubric criteria points that an agent completes successfully per 100k inference tokens." ([Tenet update](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/post-training-update-harvey-tenet))
- Result: "Our model scores 190.8 in our intelligence-per-token metric against 129.3 for the best frontier configuration and 37.2 for equivalently sized models." ([Tenet update](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/post-training-update-harvey-tenet))
- In this library: not taken as a routing rule. The router admits a model on its success rate against the task's bar and only then compares cost, so one number that folds success and tokens together could rank a cheaper model that misses the bar above one that clears it. The tokens a model spends already enter its cost through its mean tokens per call.

### Your own trained model is a candidate, kept to the tasks it was measured on

- Mechanism: Harvey trains models for single jobs and exposes them to its main model: "These capabilities are each trained separately and can be deployed as tools and sub-agents that our model can route to in order to tackle specific tasks." ([Tenet update](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/post-training-update-harvey-tenet))
- Result: its Firm Knowledge model "completes tasks competitively with leading frontier models while reducing cost per query by 90%." ([Tenet update](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/post-training-update-harvey-tenet)) The gains are uneven: "Small regressions in low-representation domains suggest a clear direction for data balancing and targeted training." ([legal agent](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/training-a-legal-agent-with-applied-compute)) Training has its own cost: "The model was trained on approximately 150 NVIDIA B300 GPUs over the course of 2 months." ([Tenet update](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/post-training-update-harvey-tenet))
- In this library: a model is a registry row priced from a vendor's list price. The changes: a registry row for a model you host, priced from your own hourly cost and throughput instead of a vendor page, and a task scope, so a model built for some tasks is never a candidate on others, not even at cold start. Task slices, which the router reads today, let a team check such a model on each part of a task where its gains may differ.

### Choose a cheap judge by agreement with strong judges, then batch its criteria

- Mechanism: two strong graders define the labels where they agree, "GPT-5.5 xhigh and Opus 4.8 Max agreed on more than 95% of criteria across runs. We treated that agreement set as the frontier consensus" ([legal agent](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/training-a-legal-agent-with-applied-compute)), and cheaper graders are scored against it and on "a 98.9% per-criterion self-consistency floor across three reruns" ([legal agent](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/training-a-legal-agent-with-applied-compute))
- Result: "GPT-5 Mini and Sonnet preserved most of the frontier signal, staying above 97% alignment with the consensus." ([legal agent](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/training-a-legal-agent-with-applied-compute)) Then "Switching to GPT-5 Mini and batching criteria produced 40x cost reduction at 16 criteria per group and 100x reduction when all criteria were grouped together." ([legal agent](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/training-a-legal-agent-with-applied-compute))
- In this library: judge calls are its first case, and it calibrates a judge on labeled items. The number of criteria per call is measured today as a setting of the judge, a variant with its own stats priced per item, as in the lesson on measured setups below. The changes: labels built from the items where every run of the chosen strong judges agrees, for a team with no human labels; and a self-consistency minimum, so a judge whose reruns disagree is excluded whatever its bound.

### The orchestrator gets the strong model; the readers can be cheap

- Mechanism: a root model hands the reading of a large data room to sub-agents and writes the report from their results.
- Result: "Holding the sub-agent model fixed while varying the root model, the gap between the highest- and lowest-scoring roots averaged about 38 percentage points." ([M&A diligence agents](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/post-training-rlm-agents-for-m-and-a-diligence)) Against that, "Holding the root fixed while varying the sub-agents, the corresponding gap between sub-agent models averaged about 8 points" ([M&A diligence agents](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/post-training-rlm-agents-for-m-and-a-diligence)), while "With Opus 5 orchestrating, for example, the root accounts for 3.8% of input tokens and 1.1% of output tokens" ([M&A diligence agents](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/post-training-rlm-agents-for-m-and-a-diligence))
- In this library: the delegation decision chooses, per task, inline work or a sub-agent and the sub-agent's model. The change: route the root and sub-agent models as a pair, each pair admitted on its own runs and priced by each side's tokens, so a strong root with cheap readers can be chosen directly.

### Under-delegation is a failure you can measure, and depth needs a cap

- Mechanism: Harvey forced delegation with a harness, then trained the root model to delegate.
- Result: without the harness, "In the traces, both agents stop reading early and write shorter memos, and neither spawns sub-agents despite having the ability to." ([M&A diligence agents](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/post-training-rlm-agents-for-m-and-a-diligence)) With it, "Across seven models, the RLM harness raises mean pass rate from 23.3% to 62.4%, a gain of 39.1 percentage points." ([M&A diligence agents](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/post-training-rlm-agents-for-m-and-a-diligence)), at a price: "Moving to the RLM harness raises generation cost per data room for six of the seven baseline models" ([M&A diligence agents](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/post-training-rlm-agents-for-m-and-a-diligence)) After training, "The post-trained root makes 64% more sub-agent calls per data room." ([M&A diligence agents](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/post-training-rlm-agents-for-m-and-a-diligence)) A second level of sub-agents failed: "In four of the ten regressions, the depth-2 run reads the data room but never writes the report." ([M&A diligence agents](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/post-training-rlm-agents-for-m-and-a-diligence))
- In this library: the delegation decision says when to delegate, but nothing checks that the agent then did. The changes: log the advised mode beside the mode the agent took and report the share of advised hand-offs it skipped, and a cap on delegation depth.

### Effort, harness and batch size are part of the measured setup

- Mechanism: Harvey fixes the reasoning effort and the harness of a comparison, "All models are evaluated at minimal reasoning effort to meet product latency requirements." ([Review Table models](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/training-frontier-review-table-models-with-applied-compute))
- Result: for two Qwen3.6 models trained to nearly the same answer score, one in an agentic retrieval harness and one in a single-turn harness, "The agentic model reached 0.799, compared to 0.794 with the single-turn model and harness." ([Review Table models](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/training-frontier-review-table-models-with-applied-compute)) Then "At this matched level of answer quality, the agentic harness used 50.3% fewer input tokens and 28.6% fewer output tokens" ([Review Table models](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/training-frontier-review-table-models-with-applied-compute)) One harness does not suit every model: "We found that our approach had a mixed effect on other models, with some scoring better (Fable 5: +7.7%) and some regressing (GPT-5.6 Sol: -4.2%)." ([Tenet update](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/post-training-update-harvey-tenet))
- In this library: a registry row can name variants of its model, such as an effort or a harness, and each is a route of its own, `vendor/model@variant`, with its own stats, so today the runs of one model at two efforts or in two harnesses no longer pool into one success rate and one mean cost. A variant keeps its model's list prices, so its cost differs by the tokens it is measured to use. A variant can also set how many items one call grades, `items_per_call`, and is then priced per item, its measured call cost over that number, so grading many criteria per call is measured as a setup of its own. A variant can also carry provider options, such as an effort parameter, which the executor sends to the provider on each call of its route. On LiveCodeBench's public runs of 5 models at two or three reasoning settings, routed as 13 variants, one model's settings are up to 0.088 apart in held-out success, and the highest setting is not always the best route ([LiveCodeBench variant results](../evidence/livecodebench-variants/RESULTS.md)); those runs record no cost.

### Latency moves work to smaller models

- Mechanism: for high-volume features Harvey takes faster models, "And sometimes the right model is not the largest one. For high-volume product surfaces like Vault, the challenge is balancing quality with latency." ([multi-model by design](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/why-harvey-is-multi-model-by-design))
- Result: at the other end, "Frontier intelligence is expensive to run. On LAB, reaching the top of the closed-source leaderboard runs to roughly $50 per task and over 20 minutes of latency." ([open legal agents](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/post-training-open-legal-agents-with-baseten-research))
- In this library: the monitor reports p50 and p95 latency per group, and a task sets a timeout per call, but no task can exclude a model for being too slow. The change: a latency bar per task on each model's measured p95, applied like the success bar.

### Switch models only on locked data and a significant result

- Mechanism: "once a collection of evaluation samples is “published,” it becomes locked, ensuring that iterative experiments have a fixed baseline for comparing new features, models, or methods." ([scaling evaluation](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/scaling-ai-evaluation-through-expertise)) Changes also pass nightly checks: "We run a suite of lightweight canary evaluations nightly to validate the day's code changes before they go to production" ([scaling evaluation](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/scaling-ai-evaluation-through-expertise))
- Result: in one side-by-side comparison of GPT-4.1 with GPT-4o, rated on anonymized answers, a test of its own and not a result of the locked sets, "The mean rating improved by over 10% (from 5.10 to 5.63), and the median score rose from 5 to 6" ([scaling evaluation](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/scaling-ai-evaluation-through-expertise)), and "the feedback helped validate our decision to shift more workloads to GPT-4.1" ([scaling evaluation](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/scaling-ai-evaluation-through-expertise))
- In this library: the Wilson bound needs evidence before a model is admitted, but the stats do not record which labeled dataset they came from, so a challenger and the model it would replace can be compared on different labels. The change: record the labeled file's hash with the stats, and an advisory paired check of a challenger against the current route on the same items before a route changes.

### Tenant policy and provider health are routing inputs

- Mechanism: "Harvey allows workspace administrators to determine which models are available to their organization." ([multi-model by design](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/why-harvey-is-multi-model-by-design)) Availability differs by region: "Opus 4.6 is available to our customers in Australia, where certain other frontier models have not yet been deployed." ([multi-model by design](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/why-harvey-is-multi-model-by-design)) A user can "pick the particular model used by Harvey’s model systems to support their work, or choose “auto” to have Harvey select for them." ([model offerings](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/expanding-harveys-model-offerings))
- Result: "If one provider experiences capacity constraints, service degradation, or an outage, Harvey can route work to an alternative model without disrupting the user's workflow." ([multi-model by design](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/why-harvey-is-multi-model-by-design))
- In this library: the executor moves to the next model of the route when a call fails or the caller's check rejects its answer, and a task can exclude model families. The changes: a policy of allowed models, families and region, applied before cost so that a fallback never leaves the allowed set, and a pinned call that skips the ranking but keeps the hard checks, with the pin logged.

## Uber

### Benchmark the real work, then take the cheapest setup that holds quality

- Mechanism: Uber builds each managed agent's benchmark from that agent's own work, "Build a benchmark out of the agent’s real work." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)), and moves to the setup with the best trade-off: "For us, Pareto efficient means cost/completed task, output quality, and model reliability." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)) For its code-review agent it scores "precision, recall, and F1 against those bugs, plus cost per review, latency, timeouts, and noise." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/))
- Result: "As shown in Figure 5, switching models improved our F1 while dramatically reducing cost/PR." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)) By the figure's labels, the model in production, Frontier Model C, costs $0.47 a review at F1 0.50, against $2.42 at F1 0.483 for Frontier Model A, and the cheapest point, Open-Weight Model C, costs $0.06 at F1 0.31; the figure does not name the models. On routing, the post's plans say: "Effective model routing relies heavily on comprehensive evaluation, given that model capabilities vary widely." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/))
- In this library: it does this per task today. A task's labeled items are its benchmark, its bar is the quality to hold, and `cost_effective` takes the cheapest model whose Wilson lower bound clears the bar, so the bar picks one point of the cost and quality frontier. Its quality is a success rate on the task's acceptance check, not F1, and latency is not part of admission yet: the latency bar in the Harvey lessons above is the planned change.

### Hold the model fixed, and count cost per finished outcome

- Mechanism: "isolating our own optimization gains means holding one model fixed, since behavior shifts with every upgrade and model family." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)) Each managed agent is tracked by "Outcome-denominated cost (cost per merged PR, cost per review, cost per alert, cost per cleanup)" ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)) beside a quality signal, to show "whether quality holds through model migrations" ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/))
- Result: from February to July, with one model held fixed, "cost per 1,000 model requests is down almost 34% from its peak, and cost per session is down 52% from its June peak." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)) The session figures start at the end of May.
- In this library: `report` sums cost, calls, failed calls and fallbacks per model, task, flavor, family or a tag the caller sets on each call, so one model's spend reads on its own, and calls tagged with a session or a work item give spend per tag. It reads no dates, though each record has a timestamp, so comparing two periods means splitting the log first. With an audit file, `report --audits` adds each group's cost per success, every attempt of its calls charged, fallbacks included, and its successes per 100,000 tokens, both `n/a` until every call of the group is audited; an `ok` outcome no audit confirmed never counts as a success, and `--by tag:<key>` gives the same per tag. A finished unit of several calls, such as a merged pull request, is not one success here: the audit marks calls, not units.

### Default sub-agents to a cheaper model

- Mechanism: "Because subagents perform well-defined tasks with specified inputs that often do not require frontier-level reasoning, we default them to a weaker, more cost-effective model while still allowing manual overrides." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)) The main model "handles task decomposition and evaluation while subagents execute the work." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/))
- Result: "The subagent default setting has proven to be the most impactful lever, and its significance continues to grow." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)) The post gives no number for it.
- In this library: the delegation decision does this for each piece of work today. The sub-agent's model is the router's pick for the task with the brief added to its input, held to the task's bar, so a cheaper model takes the work where it clears the bar and the parent keeps its own model; only a task set to take the best available model when none clears may get one below the bar, and the decision then says it is below the bar. A manual override is the pinned call planned in the Harvey lesson on tenant policy.

### Reasoning effort and context size are settings to measure

- Mechanism: two defaults of Uber's interactive tools, "Automatic compaction is triggered at 400k tokens even for 1M context window models" ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)) and "Reasoning effort defaulted to Medium" ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/))
- Result: "Our measurements show a meaningful reduction in fleet-wide input tokens per request." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)) and "For a large category of tasks, Medium reasoning hits a good balance between cost vs quality." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)) The post gives a number for neither.
- In this library: as in the Harvey lesson on measured setups, a variant of a model, an effort or a compaction threshold, is a route with its own stats today, so its runs do not pool with the model's other runs. The library records the threshold as a label of the variant and compacts nothing itself. A compaction threshold bounds the context, not the spend. On LiveCodeBench's public runs, `openai/o4-mini` solves 0.833, 0.893 and 0.909 of held-out problems at low, medium and high effort, and `openai/o3-mini-2025-01-31` 0.776, 0.822 and 0.819, with no cost recorded ([LiveCodeBench variant results](../evidence/livecodebench-variants/RESULTS.md)).

### Price cache writes, and set the cache's life by how the work pauses

- Mechanism: cached reads cost less and writes cost more, "reducing subsequent reads to just 0.1x the standard input token rate. However, write premiums vary: 5-minute cache entries cost 1.25x, while 1-hour entries cost 2x." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)) So the choice of cache life "depends on the duration of gaps between turns." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/))
- Result: "Because engineers often leave interactive sessions idle for more than 5 minutes, we transitioned from the default 5-minute TTL to a 1-hour window." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)) Sub-agents keep the short one: "Sub-agents, by contrast, retain a 5-minute cache TTL because their execution focus is limited to single, short-lived tasks." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)) The post gives no saving for the change.
- In this library: the one cache price it reads is the cached-read ratio of the delegation decision and its evaluator, which prices the parent's reads of its context in inline work at a share of its list input price. Model choice uses list input prices, and no cache write is priced. The change: registry prices for cached reads and cache writes, and measured cached and written tokens per task and model, so a model's price on a task follows its measured hit rate, and a write premium can make caching lose on one-shot work.

### Keep tool schemas and polling out of the model's context

- Mechanism: with every tool's schema loaded up front, "with over 100 tools installed, this pre-loading added approximately 50K-70K tokens of schema overhead to the initial prompt, which was subsequently re-sent on every context turn." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)) Uber lets the model search for tools and call them as shell commands, and runs recurring tool work in scripts: "the loop runs in a subprocess, and only the summary comes back." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/))
- Result: on "5 identical SQL queries through both paths in the same session" ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)), "even for minimal result sets far below response-size limits, code-mode reduces token usage by more than 50%." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)) For bulk work, "the savings compound to more than 90%." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)) And "By deploying more than 25 pre-built code-mode skills for our most-accessed MCP servers, we ensure standard workflows default to the most cost-effective path." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)) The counts are tokens, not dollars, and the post gives no quality figure for the scripts.
- In this library: it routes model calls and prices their tokens. A prompt's size, tool schemas included, enters through the tokens per call it measures and the context-window check, and a script that does recurring work is outside its choice. The change: a fixed procedure for recurring work as a route candidate beside the model routes, admitted on its own measured runs against the same bar and priced by the model calls it makes plus its tool cost, so it wins only where it clears the bar.

### Ground the agent before it searches

- Mechanism: "An ungrounded agent fails slowly rather than cheaply, repeatedly sending an expanding context window to search one more location." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)) Uber built a graph of its code and data that any agent can query.
- Result: in one comparison, the same prompt to the same model, the grounded agent "delivered the answer in 38 seconds" ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)), while the ungrounded one "spent 20 minutes inspecting service code, spawning 2 subagents, and hitting 3 errors before incorrectly concluding the dataset was unqueryable." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)) It is one example, not a measured rate.
- In this library: not taken as routing behavior, since what a prompt holds is the caller's. A grounded and an ungrounded prompt can be measured today as two named variants of one model, each with its own success rate and cost, the caller's harness building each prompt.

### Find the routing waste in the logs

- Mechanism: a session dashboard reads each user's session traces and "flags 16 distinct anti-patterns across sessions, pairing each with its financial impact and a targeted remediation." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/))
- Result: the first category the post names is "Suboptimal model routing: Executing simple multi-turn sessions on Opus that Sonnet could easily fulfill." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)) The post gives no total for what the dashboard found.
- In this library: `report` shows spend, failures and fallbacks per group, and `below_bar_calls` counts the calls answered by a model under the bar, but it does not say where a cheaper route would have done the work. The change: a savings report per task from the log, with calls per day, share of spend, cost per success and the daily saving if a cheaper route cleared the bar, its savings labeled hypothetical.

### Spend tiers and nudges, not strict caps

- Mechanism: in August, "To avoid imposing strict caps, we implemented real-time spend tracking and automated nudges" ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)): a live cost counter, "One shared tier across all interactive harnesses, not per-tool budgets." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)), separate tiers for managed agents, "Alerts at 50/80/100% of expected spend so engineers have time to plan." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/)), and a manager's sign-off for a higher tier. In June, Bloomberg reported caps per tool: "The rideshare giant is limiting all employees to $1,500 in monthly token spending per AI coding tool, an Uber spokesperson said in response to a Bloomberg News inquiry." ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-02/uber-caps-usage-of-ai-tools-like-claude-code-to-cut-costs)) And "That means spending on one tool doesn’t have a bearing on the budget for another." ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-02/uber-caps-usage-of-ai-tools-like-claude-code-to-cut-costs))
- Result: neither source gives a saving for its policy; the post credits the stabilized total spend to "optimizations across the board." ([software factory](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/efficient-software-factory/))
- In this library: no budget today. The change: a budget with a scope (a tag, a task or one run), a window and a mode: warn at set shares of it, move only to routes that still clear the bar, or refuse the call. Fallbacks and delegated calls count to the same budget, a context limit is never a spend limit, and no call is routed below the bar to stay inside a budget.

## Other teams

Write-ups by other teams and projects that run language models in production, or that build routing for those who do, and two analyses of what model prices do over time. This version reads four on prompt caching, where a provider serves a repeated prompt prefix from its cache at a fraction of its input price; three on routing, panels and a distilled model: LMSYS's learned router, OpenRouter's panels and Replit's code-repair model; and four on gateways, evaluation and price drift: Grab's gateway in front of its model providers, GitHub's evaluation of models for Copilot, and a16z's and Epoch AI's analyses of how fast the price of a given benchmark score falls. The three on routing, panels and a distilled model give results on benchmarks, not on production traffic: RouteLLM's are a research lab's, Fusion's a vendor's on its own product, and Replit's from two evaluation sets of its own, one sampled from its users' code. The two on price drift read public list prices and benchmark scores, not any team's bill.

### The cache hit rate is a cost number

- Mechanism: an agent sends its whole context again at every step, so its input tokens far outnumber its output tokens, and the share of them served from the cache sets the bill. Manus: "If I had to choose just one metric, I'd argue that the KV-cache hit rate is the single most important metric for a production-stage AI agent." ([Manus](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus)) Its numbers: "In Manus, for example, the average input-to-output token ratio is around 100:1." ([Manus](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus)) and "A typical task in Manus requires around 50 tool calls on average." ([Manus](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus))
- Result: the price gap Manus gives: "with Claude Sonnet, for instance, cached input tokens cost 0.30 USD/MTok, while uncached ones cost 3 USD/MTok" ([Manus](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus)). Kern AI measured "a real agent running 100k+ token prompts on Anthropic (Claude Opus via OpenRouter)" ([Kern AI](https://kern-ai.com/blog/prompt-caching)): "Before caching, a typical multi-step turn cost ~600k input tokens at full price." ([Kern AI](https://kern-ai.com/blog/prompt-caching)) "After: ~600k tokens, but ~590k of them are cached at 10% cost." ([Kern AI](https://kern-ai.com/blog/prompt-caching)) Its table gives a hit rate of 99% for the tool calls inside a turn, 85 to 95% between turns, and 0% for one turn after a new segment of the conversation.
- In this library: model choice prices every input token at the model's list input price, and the one cache price it reads is the cached-read ratio of the delegation decision. The change is the one the Uber lesson on cache writes names: registry prices for cached reads and cache writes, and measured cached and written tokens per task and model, so a model's price on a task follows its own measured hit rate, and a model that is cheaper per token but misses the cache can lose to a pricier one that hits it.

### Keep the prompt prefix the same, byte for byte

- Mechanism: a cached prefix serves only an exact match. Manus: "even a single-token difference can invalidate the cache from that token onward." ([Manus](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus)) "A common mistake is including a timestamp" ([Manus](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus)). On tools: "unless absolutely necessary, avoid dynamically adding or removing tools mid-iteration." ([Manus](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus)), since tool definitions sit near the front of the context: "So any change will invalidate the KV-cache for all subsequent actions and observations." ([Manus](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus)) Kern AI names three causes of misses in an agent, a context window trimmed at a point that moves each turn, a summary rewritten into the system prompt and a cache marker that moves each turn, and pins its three cache breakpoints to stable positions.
- Result: with its last breakpoint on the last user message, inside a turn "every step after the first reuses the entire prefix up to BP3 at 99%+ hit rates." ([Kern AI](https://kern-ai.com/blog/prompt-caching)) Neradot on the opposite case: "Add cache_control to a prompt with a timestamp in the header and nothing is ever read back, because the prefix differs every time" ([Neradot](https://www.neradot.com/post/cutting-inference-cost-36-percent-with-prompt-caching)).
- In this library: it builds no prompts, so the prompt layout stays with the caller and the library does not take this lesson as routing. What it can do is show a broken prefix: the planned cache prices read measured cached tokens and never assume a hit from a repeated prompt, and a planned monitor check flags a task and model whose hit rate stays under a floor the task sets for a run of calls. Since a cache holds the prefix one model was sent, a planned change also prices a call moved to another model mid-session at that model's uncached input price, and keeps a pricier model when the switch would cost more.

### Shrink the prompt first, then cache what is read often

- Mechanism: Neradot's support agent sent a fixed part of about 14,000 tokens (rules and tool schemas), a part of about 3,500 for each ticket and a growing part for each turn, and "Tier 1 is 76% of the input on the first request of a ticket." ([Neradot](https://www.neradot.com/post/cutting-inference-cost-36-percent-with-prompt-caching)) It first had a model cut the repetition out of the prompt, read the diff by hand, and then put cache breakpoints at the end of the fixed part and of the ticket part.
- Result: the trim: "That got us 15% fewer tokens, on prompts we already thought were tight." ([Neradot](https://www.neradot.com/post/cutting-inference-cost-36-percent-with-prompt-caching)) The ticket part's cache entry was read only once or twice before the ticket ended, so its breakpoint saved, in Neradot's words, "call it 6% on a good day" ([Neradot](https://www.neradot.com/post/cutting-inference-cost-36-percent-with-prompt-caching)), and "We kept the tier 1 breakpoint and dropped the tier 2 one." ([Neradot](https://www.neradot.com/post/cutting-inference-cost-36-percent-with-prompt-caching)) "Pass two came out at 25% off what pass one left us." ([Neradot](https://www.neradot.com/post/cutting-inference-cost-36-percent-with-prompt-caching)) In all: "36% total, with the same outputs and the same eval scores." ([Neradot](https://www.neradot.com/post/cutting-inference-cost-36-percent-with-prompt-caching)) Its rule: "what matters is how often a block is read, not how big it is." ([Neradot](https://www.neradot.com/post/cutting-inference-cost-36-percent-with-prompt-caching))
- In this library: a call's price uses the task's mean tokens per call, with no split between the prefix its calls share and the part each call adds, so the library cannot tell which tasks have a prefix worth caching. The change: the planned measured cached and written tokens per task and model give that split as the provider served it, and a cache write priced at its premium shows how little caching saves on a part read only once or twice before it expires, as Neradot's ticket part was, and that a part written and never read loses.

### Set the cache's life by the traffic rate

- Mechanism: a cache entry lives five minutes by default and each read keeps it alive, so at peak it never expires, and off-peak the calls come further apart than its life. Neradot: "during slow hours, the cache hit rate collapsed." ([Neradot](https://www.neradot.com/post/cutting-inference-cost-36-percent-with-prompt-caching)) Its provider also offers a one-hour life at a write premium of 2x instead of 1.25x.
- Result: for a slow hour with tickets 12 minutes apart, Neradot works out that the one-hour life gives "roughly a 2× improvement on tier 1 off-peak" ([Neradot](https://www.neradot.com/post/cutting-inference-cost-36-percent-with-prompt-caching)), while "At peak the same switch would cost us money" ([Neradot](https://www.neradot.com/post/cutting-inference-cost-36-percent-with-prompt-caching)). It picked no life by traffic: "We haven't built it" ([Neradot](https://www.neradot.com/post/cutting-inference-cost-36-percent-with-prompt-caching)). Uber chose the longer life for interactive sessions, as its lesson on cache life says.
- In this library: the monitor's records carry a time stamp, so the gap between a task's calls is in its log, but no report reads it, and no cache write is priced. The library does not take the choice of cache life, a provider setting the caller makes; with cache writes priced and cached and written tokens measured per task and model, as planned, a task's reads per write show whether a longer life pays.

### Caching that every layer seems to support can still miss, and an alert does not stop spend

- Mechanism: one developer ran a local coding agent that reached Claude Opus 4.6 through an OpenAI-compatible API, a proxy and a cloud provider's hosted model service: "I assumed prompt caching was working because every layer in the chain made that assumption feel reasonable" ([Hacker News post](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47933355)). The cache mostly missed: "“Prompt caching is supported” is not the same as “your actual agent stack is using prompt caching correctly.”" ([Hacker News post](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47933355))
- Result: "The gross Opus usage was $37,901.73." ([Hacker News post](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47933355)) Two of its three input lines: "uncached input tokens: ~6.47B tokens, ~$35.6k" ([Hacker News post](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47933355)) and "cache read input tokens: ~1.67B tokens, ~$918" ([Hacker News post](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47933355)), so uncached input is about 94% of the gross, computed here; with the third, "cache write input tokens: ~101M tokens, ~$698" ([Hacker News post](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47933355)), about 20% of the input tokens were read from the cache, also computed here. On the alerts: "“Budget alerts are configured” is not the same as “spend will stop.”" ([Hacker News post](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47933355))
- In this library: the monitor records each attempt's input and output tokens and its cost, with no cached or written tokens, and there is no budget. The change: cached and written tokens in the attempt records, as planned for cache prices; a monitor check that flags a run of calls whose hit rate stays under the task's floor; and an in-process budget that refuses a call, or moves it only to a route that still clears the bar, as in the Uber lesson on spend tiers.

### A learned router's saving depends on the task and on the data it learned from

- Mechanism: LMSYS trained routers that send each query to one of two models, "a stronger, more expensive model, and a weaker but cheaper model." ([RouteLLM](https://lmsys.org/blog/2024-07-01-routellm/)), learning from Chatbot Arena's votes, where "Each data point consists of a prompt and a comparison between the response quality of two models on that prompt" ([RouteLLM](https://lmsys.org/blog/2024-07-01-routellm/)).
- Result: "cost reductions of over 85% on MT Bench, 45% on MMLU, and 35% on GSM8K as compared to using only GPT-4, while still achieving 95% of GPT-4’s performance." ([RouteLLM](https://lmsys.org/blog/2024-07-01-routellm/)) Trained on the Arena data alone, on MMLU "all routers perform poorly at a near-random level when trained only on the Arena dataset, which we attribute to most MMLU questions being out-of-distribution." ([RouteLLM](https://lmsys.org/blog/2024-07-01-routellm/)) With about 1,500 labelled MMLU questions added, the best router needed "only 54% GPT-4 calls to achieve 95% of GPT-4 performance, 14% cheaper than the random baseline." ([RouteLLM](https://lmsys.org/blog/2024-07-01-routellm/)) The same routers, not retrained, kept "performance comparable to our original model pair" ([RouteLLM](https://lmsys.org/blog/2024-07-01-routellm/)) on a new pair, on MT Bench.
- In this library: it does not take a router that predicts, for each query, which model will answer it well; v1 routes per task, and such a router is one of its non-goals. What it does is what the MMLU result argues for: a model is admitted on its own success rate measured on the task's own labelled items, while a leaderboard score only orders the candidates, and where a task mixes easy and hard items, task slices give each part its own measured rate. A task's bar is a success rate the caller sets, not a share of the strongest model's score, and the committed evidence gives each result per dataset and per bar.

### A panel of models can beat the best single model: score both on the same items, at their cost

- Mechanism: "we dispatch it to a panel of models in parallel, each with web search and web fetch enabled. A judge model reads every panel response and produces structured analysis" ([Fusion](https://openrouter.ai/blog/announcements/fusion-beats-frontier/)), and the model called writes the answer from that analysis.
- Result: on 100 DRACO deep-research tasks, "A budget panel (Gemini 3 Flash, Kimi K2.6, and DeepSeek V4 Pro) beat GPT-5.5 and Opus 4.8. It came within 1% of Fable 5’s score while being 50% of the cost." ([Fusion](https://openrouter.ai/blog/announcements/fusion-beats-frontier/)) A panel of two frontier models scored higher still: "Fable 5 + GPT-5.5 fused together scored 69.0%" ([Fusion](https://openrouter.ai/blog/announcements/fusion-beats-frontier/)), against 65.3% for Fable 5 alone, both on 93 of the tasks. Opus 4.8 paired with itself scored 65.5%, "a 6.7-point jump over solo Opus 4.8 (58.8%)" ([Fusion](https://openrouter.ai/blog/announcements/fusion-beats-frontier/)), so "a meaningful chunk of Fusion’s lift comes from the synthesis step itself" ([Fusion](https://openrouter.ai/blog/announcements/fusion-beats-frontier/)). A panel is slower, "a multi-step process that is often 2-3x longer than a standard call." ([Fusion](https://openrouter.ai/blog/announcements/fusion-beats-frontier/)), and for coding the vendor has the coding model call it only when a question is worth it: "The base model handles routine coding directly and can choose to call Fusion selectively on questions worth spending more time and money to get a thorough answer" ([Fusion](https://openrouter.ai/blog/announcements/fusion-beats-frontier/)).
- In this library: the offline evaluator scores the routed choice, the cheapest model, the best model on calibration and each model on the same held-out items, beside an `oracle` that counts an item correct when any model is, at the lowest cost among the correct ones: an upper bound on choosing among the models' own answers item by item, not a panel, since a panel pays every member and a judge, and its judge writes a new answer that the dataset has not scored. It refuses a matrix in which some model lacks an item, so no model is scored on fewer items than another, as Fable 5 was here. It scores no panel. The change: a panel bound in the evaluator, an item correct when any of the k cheapest admitted routes is, with all k charged, printed beside the `oracle` as a bound and never as a panel to deploy; a real panel is scored only on a dataset that labels its fused answers, and a race only on one that holds every billed output, and a refusal or a content-filter block counts as a failure. A cascade, in the library today, pays for a stronger model only on a signal, the selective use the post advises.

### Train a small model of your own for a narrow, high-volume job

- Mechanism: Replit chose a job with data to spare, "LSP diagnostics among our most common events, with hundreds of millions per day" ([code repair](https://blog.replit.com/code-repair)), while "only 10% of LSP diagnostic messages in Python projects on Replit have associated fixes." ([code repair](https://blog.replit.com/code-repair)) It had large models write fixes, kept those that applied and that another model judged correct, and trained a 7B model on them: "We distill a model from synthesized diffs because fixed errors taken directly from user data are noisier than synthesized diffs." ([code repair](https://blog.replit.com/code-repair)) On its size: "We chose the model size of 7B to balance model capabilities with our constraints of inference latency and cost." ([code repair](https://blog.replit.com/code-repair))
- Result: "Replit Code Repair 7B is competitive with models much larger on both evaluation benchmarks." ([code repair](https://blog.replit.com/code-repair)) On the set sampled from its users' code: "There is a large gap between the performance of Replit Code Repair 7B and other models (except GPT-4 Turbo)." ([code repair](https://blog.replit.com/code-repair)) That set was the harder one: "The overall performance of models on our real-world eval remains low when compared to the Leetcode repair eval" ([code repair](https://blog.replit.com/code-repair)). The scores of each model are in chart images only.
- In this library: as in the Harvey lesson on your own trained model, a model is a registry row priced from a vendor's list price, so a hosted 7B model has no price of its own, and nothing keeps a model trained for one job off the others. The changes named there cover it: a registry row priced from your own hourly cost and throughput, and a task scope. Which job to train for is the caller's; the planned savings report, run with a target cost per call, would show by task the daily saving if a route at that cost cleared the bar, and how many labelled items it would need to be admitted. Replit's set drawn from its own users is what the library admits a model on: its success rate measured on the task's own labelled items.

### Count cost per request and per service, and keep batch work off online work's limits

- Mechanism: Grab's AI Gateway is a set of reverse proxies in front of external model providers, and every call goes through it. "The gateway performs cost calculations for each request once the response is received from the provider." ([Grab](https://engineering.grab.com/grab-ai-gateway)) "Finally, a job aggregates the cost for each service which is used for reporting on dashboards and showback." ([Grab](https://engineering.grab.com/grab-ai-gateway)) And "alerts are configured to notify if a service exceeds the cost threshold." ([Grab](https://engineering.grab.com/grab-ai-gateway)) On limits: "AI Gateway enforces its own rate limit on top of the global provider limits to make sure quotas are not consumed by a single service." ([Grab](https://engineering.grab.com/grab-ai-gateway)) and "Currently, limits are enforced on the request rate at the key level." ([Grab](https://engineering.grab.com/grab-ai-gateway))
- Result: the post gives no cost figure; its numbers are of scale, "With more than 300 unique use cases onboarded and many of those making it to production, AI Gateway has gained popularity since its inception in 2023." ([Grab](https://engineering.grab.com/grab-ai-gateway)) On the limits: "As adoption increases, we have encountered issues where batch usage interfered with the uptime of online services." ([Grab](https://engineering.grab.com/grab-ai-gateway)) and "The use of Async APIs does mitigate the issues, but not all use cases can adhere to turnaround time." ([Grab](https://engineering.grab.com/grab-ai-gateway)) Its plan: "More advanced rate limiting policies based on token usage or daily/monthly running costs should be introduced to enforce better and fairer limits." ([Grab](https://engineering.grab.com/grab-ai-gateway))
- In this library: the monitor records each attempt's cost, and `report --by tag:<key>` sums spend, failures and fallbacks by a tag the caller passes with each call, such as a team or a service, so showback per service needs no gateway. It has no budget and no rate limit. The changes: the in-process budget of the Uber lesson on spend tiers; and request and token limits per route in the registry, with the work a task marks as batch held to a share of a route's limit, so it moves to the next route that clears the bar instead of taking the online work's quota. Both hold inside one process; a limit shared by many services still needs a gateway or the provider's own.

### A catalogue of models does not pick one, and a stand-in model must clear the same bar

- Mechanism: the gateway gives every provider one API, so "Providers can be switched simply by changing the “model” parameter in the API request." ([Grab](https://engineering.grab.com/grab-ai-gateway)) It also moves calls between models to use capacity bought ahead: "It provides the control points to dynamically route requests for certain models to a different albeit similar model backed by a reserved instance." ([Grab](https://engineering.grab.com/grab-ai-gateway))
- Result: the post gives no saving for either. The choice of model became its users' problem: "With over 50 AI models each suited for a specific task type, finding the correct model to use is becoming complex." ([Grab](https://engineering.grab.com/grab-ai-gateway)) "Users are often unsure of the difference between models in terms of capabilities, latency, and cost implications." ([Grab](https://engineering.grab.com/grab-ai-gateway)) Its planned answer is a catalogue listing each model's "input/output modality, token limits, provider quota, pricing, and reference guide" ([Grab](https://engineering.grab.com/grab-ai-gateway)).
- In this library: the registry is that catalogue for prices, context windows and model families, and the caller names a task, not a model: the router reads each model's measured success rate on the task and takes the cheapest that clears the task's bar, so no user has to tell fifty models apart. A stand-in is routed only when it clears the same bar on its own measured rate; a similar model is not taken to be as good. The library prices a model per token only, so capacity bought ahead, a fixed cost, has no price in it; the registry row priced from an hourly cost and throughput, planned in the Harvey lesson on your own trained model, is the form such a price would take. The latency part of the choice is the latency bar planned in the Harvey lesson on latency.

### Test a model before the switch, and re-run the tests on the models in production every day

- Mechanism: "We run more than 4,000 offline tests, most of them as part of our automated CI pipeline." ([GitHub](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/generative-ai/how-we-evaluate-models-for-github-copilot/)) For code, "We have a collection of around 100 containerized repositories that have passed a battery of CI tests." ([GitHub](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/generative-ai/how-we-evaluate-models-for-github-copilot/)), broken on purpose to see whether a model makes the tests pass again; for chat, "a collection of more than 1,000 technical questions" ([GitHub](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/generative-ai/how-we-evaluate-models-for-github-copilot/)), the harder ones graded by another model: "We use a model with known good performance for these purposes to ensure consistent evaluations across our work." ([GitHub](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/generative-ai/how-we-evaluate-models-for-github-copilot/)) "We also routinely audit the outputs of this LLM in evaluation scenarios to make sure it’s working correctly." ([GitHub](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/generative-ai/how-we-evaluate-models-for-github-copilot/)) Before a switch, staff use the new model: "We also conduct live internal evaluations, similar to canary testing, where we switch a number of Hubbers to use a new model." ([GitHub](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/generative-ai/how-we-evaluate-models-for-github-copilot/)) The switch itself needs no client change: "We can easily change which API the proxy server calls for responses without any sort of change on the client side." ([GitHub](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/generative-ai/how-we-evaluate-models-for-github-copilot/))
- Result: "We also run these tests against our production models every day. If we see degradation, we do some auditing to find out why the models aren’t performing as well as they used to." ([GitHub](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/generative-ai/how-we-evaluate-models-for-github-copilot/)) The post gives no score, cost or count of the drops it found. On adopting a model it names a trade-off: "There are sometimes inverse relationships between metrics. Higher latency might actually lead to a higher acceptance rate because users might see fewer suggestions total." ([GitHub](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/generative-ai/how-we-evaluate-models-for-github-copilot/))
- In this library: the caller calls a task, not a model, so a route changes with the registry and the stats while the caller's code does not, as with GitHub's proxy. `calibrate` merges labeled results into one stats file of success and trial counts with no dates, so a fall in a model's recent success rate is averaged into its whole count, and README's limitations say the library has no online drift detection. Judges are its first case, calibrated on labeled items like any task. The changes: a drift check over audited production calls in a recent window, kept apart from the all-time counts, that flags a route whose bound falls under the bar or rises over it; a set share of routed calls also sent to a reference route, so a quality loss nobody labeled shows up in pairs to label; and the paired check of the Harvey lesson on locked data before a route changes. Which measure counts as success stays the caller's, and the planned latency bar would be a check of its own beside the success bar, not folded into it.

### The price of a given score falls fast and unevenly: date the prices and re-run the choice

- Mechanism: both analyses follow the cheapest model that reaches a fixed benchmark score over time. a16z: "The graph below shows the cost of the cheapest model for any month that would give us a minimum MMLU score of 42." ([a16z](https://a16z.com/llmflation-llm-inference-cost/)), with prices from the Internet Archive and "If the price differed for input and output tokens, we took the average of the two." ([a16z](https://a16z.com/llmflation-llm-inference-cost/)) Epoch AI: "We used this dataset to identify the lowest-priced LLMs that match or exceed a given score on a benchmark." ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/llm-inference-price-trends)), on six benchmarks, where "All prices were a 3:1 weighted average of input and output token prices." ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/llm-inference-price-trends))
- Result: a16z: "For an LLM of equivalent performance, the cost is decreasing by 10x every year." ([a16z](https://a16z.com/llmflation-llm-inference-cost/)) Epoch AI: "For instance, the price to achieve GPT-4’s performance on a set of PhD-level science questions fell by 40x per year." ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/llm-inference-price-trends)) "The rate of decline varies dramatically depending on the performance milestone, ranging from 9x to 900x per year." ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/llm-inference-price-trends)) And "The fastest price drops in that range have occurred in the past year, so it’s less clear that those will persist." ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/llm-inference-price-trends)) The output price of the leading model did not fall the same way, as the post put it in November 2024: "OpenAI’s leading model today, o1, has the same cost per output token as GPT-3 had at launch ($60 per million)." ([a16z](https://a16z.com/llmflation-llm-inference-cost/))
- In this library: `select` ranks the models on the prices in the registry it is given, so a newer price file can change a route the next time it runs, and the monitor logs each attempt's cost when it is made. The shipped registry gives one date for all the prices it holds, its top-level `as_of`, which no code reads. A row whose vendor states an end date for its price can carry it as `price_valid_until`, and after that day `select`, `delegate` and `evaluate` print a warning for each such model whose price the run can use; a price with no end date never warns, however old, and nothing shows which routes new prices would change. The changes: a date per registry row for when its price was read; a report column with the logged calls' spend at today's prices beside the cost they were logged at; and a check that warns on prices read longer ago than a set age and lists the tasks whose route changes at current prices. The library applies neither rate to its prices: both are the price of the cheapest model that reaches a score, not of one model over time.
